7142. Bruce Strowd 1 (Frances (Fannie) Harris Headen , Mary Edwards Rives , Edwards Rives , Thomas Henry Rives , Mary Pegram , Daniel , George ) was born 2 on 18 Aug 1891 in Orange Co., North Carolina. He died on 16 Dec 1955 in Chapel Hill, Orange Co., North Carolina.

Bruce and Mattie had two children, Robert Lee II, named for his grandfather, and Billie Atwater, no doubt named for her maternal grandfather Billy Atwater, and perhaps for her paternal greatgrandfather, William F. Strowd. The Southern Part of Heaven by William Meade Prince of Chapel Hill includes several anecdotes regarding Bruce. A favorite old Chapel Hill story is that Bruce at age fifteen or so, built an "infernal machine" using parts from his mother's sewing machine. The "Strowdmobile", sometimes referred to as the "Devil Wagon", resembled a long narrow wooden wagon with a steering wheel, complete with a small engine, a drag brake, and no muffler. It was therefore very loud and scared the horses, which in turn, of course, scared those sitting upon or behind those horses. The Town Fathers passed an ordinance specifically directed toward Bruce and his contraption, intending to force the ban which would keep him and his machine out of the business area of Chapel Hill. It did not work. It appears that even his father, a merchant and Vice President of the Bank of Chapel Hill, had little control over his youngest son, either!

The Chapel Hill newspaper still occasionally carries a story about how many thousands of times Bruce broke the law only to later become the pioneer automobile dealer of the area. Strowd Motor Company, his Ford dealership, was in business from 1914 to about 1950. In 1910 Bruce attended North Carolina State University in Raleigh, then headed north to Kenosha, Wisconsin, to work and study at the Rambler Plant. He wanted to know all about the automobile. When he returned to Chapel Hill there were thirteen vehicles in the village, and nobody but Bruce who knew how to repair them. Even his own steering wheel came off in his hands while his Model T was stalled on the railroad tract between Chapel Hill and Hillsborough. He later patented a starter.

Bruce was a charter member of the Chapel Hill Rotary Club, Chairman of the first welfare board, and one of the few lay members of the UNC Faculty Club. In 1937 he was presented a trophy by the Kiwanis Club, which designated him Chapel Hill's "Most Valuable Citizen". He rarely missed a basketball game in the old "Tin Can", and held close friendships with people of all walks of life, including UNC President, Dr. Frank Porter Graham. He opened his Chatham County farm pond for use by the Chapel Hill and Carrboro Boy Scouts and built shelters throughout northern Chatham County to protect the young people from inclement weather while waiting for their school buses. Bruce gave the opportunity to all Chapel Hill youth who wanted to take the last train ride on the old University Line, of which his grandfather, Buck Strowd, was an Incorporator.

Bruce married Mattie Lillian Atwater daughter of William Bascum Atwater and Martha Helen Clegg. Mattie was born 1 on 24 Oct 1888 in Chatham Co., North Carolina. She died 2 on 2 Nov 1972 in Chapel Hill, Orange Co., North Carolina.

The following is from Bebe Fox's family notes:

Mattie was the youngest of the eight children. Her father died when she was twenty years of age. Mattie studied at Elon College. She was known for her beautiful watercolor paintings and was also a talented pianist. In her earlier years her dresses were of the latest fashion, beaded flapper dresses and cloche hats remain. She was especially fond of small children and animals. She always had at least two fox terriers, usually named Leo and Jerry, and once she had a monkey named Jocko. There was a stone goldfish pool with a tall birdbath in the center. They also were allowed to play with the "pretties" in her dressing table drawers -costume jewelry, lacy handkerchiefs, and dainty little rouge pots. Mattie had a green thumb. She grew beautiful roses and had a way with African violets. She was a good cook and set a pretty table. Her grandchildren called her Bama.

7169. Dr. Julian Theodore Feild 1, 2, 3 (Henrietta R. Boisseau , Julia Rives , Richard Augustus Rives , Robert Rives , Mary Pegram , Daniel , George ) was born 4, 5 on 10 Jan 1846 in Louisiana. He died in Dec 1932 in Fort Worth, Tarrant Co., Texas.

The following is from a book printed in 1930 entitled Texas Medicine Man:

"The name of Dr. Julian T. Field follows closely that of Dr. Peak in the history of the doctors of Fort Worth.

Dr. Field's telephone number was No. 1.

Born on his father's plantation in Caddo Parish, Louisiana 1847, the doctor claimed he was a Texan, for while the house in which he was born was in Louisiana, very shortly after his birth the family moved to a new house on the Texas portion of the plantation.

Dr. Field was the son of Captain Julian Field and Henrietta Boisseau Field. Although born a farmer's boy, he never followed a plow or worked on the farm in any capacity. He came to Fort Worth at the age of eight years, a bright, quick, mischievous boy. He went to school to Colonel J. P. Smith in the old hospital of the fort, a box shanty which the soldiers had vacated. He soon became a leader in serenades and general frolics among the young people. The town was just an outpost of civilization. Indians, buffalo, deer, and panther were all close neighbors, but the boys and girls managed to have a good time and get an education of sorts.

Dr. Field's next school after the shanty was the Institute at Clarksville, where his teacher was the celebrated Dr. McKenzie. In 1863, at the age of seventeen, entering the Confederate service, he was elected Lieutenant of Company C., 15th Texas Cavalry. He served throughout the war.

In 1865 he began the study of medicine under Dr. C. M. Peak. In 1867 he entered the University of Louisville, Kentucky, and taking two full courses was graduated March 1869. He was then only twenty-two years old. He went to Mansfield, where he did a very laborious and extensive country practice from 1869 to 1872. Ambitious to stand at the head of his profession, he went to Bellevue Hospital New York, where he remained a year. In 1873 he returned to his boyhood home, Fort Worth. He built his own office on Weatherford Street and started practice among friends who had known and loved him as a child. They appreciated the effort he had made to prepare himself in the best schools and they gave him their fullest support and confidence.

In 1873 Dr. Field became first president of the Medical Examining Board of the district and acted as such until a new board was appointed under a new law. He was appointed in 1878, United States Examining Surgeon for Pensions. He was a charter member of the Tarrant County Medical Association and was received as a member of the State Association in 1874.

In January, 1878, he performed a triple amputation of an arm at the shoulder joint and the legs below the knees, from which the patient made a good recovery. This was a very unusual case and created most favorable comment. As a recognition of his skill, Dr. Field was made a vice-president of the State Association in 1878.

In 1869 Dr. Field married Miss Sarah M. Furguson. Four daughters were born to them.

Dr. Field was a Mason and a Knight of Pythias. He attended, with his wife, the Cumberland Presbyterian Church.

Dr. Field was one of the first doctors to recognize the value of the artesian water near Fort Worth and very strongly advised its use as a preventive of typhoid fever."

The 1900 Census shows the couple have been married for 31 years. They have had 4 children, all living. Only Willie remains at home.

His obituary lists his suvivors as four daughters: Mrs. W. R. Edrington, Mrs. J. M. Collins and Mrs. G. V. Morton, all of Fort Worth, and Mrs. J. W. Sandridge, Aledo; two sisters, Mrs. J. C. Foster, Fort Worth and Mrs. John E. Walraven, Dallas, and two brothers, Dr. Bingham Feild, Enid, Okla. and George Feild, Dallas, 12 grand children and 9 great-grandchildren.

Julian married Sarah M. Ferguson. Sarah was born in 1845 in Texas.
